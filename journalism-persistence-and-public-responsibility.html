A reporting career is built through practice

In The Untold Podcast’s second episode, a New Vision journalist describes a reporting path that includes lifestyle, sports and political coverage. The conversation is useful because it does not present a finished career as something that arrived fully formed. Different assignments, editorial feedback and setbacks all appear in the account.

He recalls wanting to move into political reporting and producing an early story that did not reach publication. Rather than treating that experience as the end of the attempt, he describes recognising the distance between his copy and the work of more experienced reporters. That is a specific kind of learning: noticing what is missing before expecting others to trust the result.

Our reading is that an unsuccessful attempt becomes valuable only when it changes the next one. For a student reporter, that could mean asking an editor whether the central question was clear, whether the evidence was strong enough or whether the opening promised something the reporting did not deliver.

Reading is part of the work

The guest discusses learning from editors, other journalists and published reporting. He describes looking at the work of others to see where his own writing could improve. His account makes persistence more concrete than simply continuing to submit the same kind of story.

For a reader hoping to develop this skill, try comparing two reports on the same subject. What does each establish? Who is heard? What remains uncertain? This is our suggested exercise, not a checklist supplied by the guest. Its purpose is to turn reading into attention to evidence, structure and the questions a story leaves unanswered.

A compelling account still needs checking

When asked about information under deadline pressure, the guest points to primary and secondary sources, background reading and speaking to people familiar with the issue. He also discusses contacting relevant authorities. The important theme is that receiving an account and establishing what can responsibly be published are different stages.

Our practical takeaway is to separate what a source says from what the reporting can demonstrate. A useful working note might have three headings: what is known, who can support it and what still needs checking. A confident speaker is not, by itself, evidence that a claim is correct. An official account also needs appropriate scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance.

That distinction matters beyond a newsroom. Before forwarding a dramatic claim in a group chat, the same habit can help: find the original source, look for the missing context and avoid presenting an allegation as an established fact.

Access should not become allegiance

Another thread is the relationship between a reporter and the people being covered. The guest warns against becoming part of the story or taking on the role of a subject’s spokesperson. He distinguishes staying close enough to obtain information from giving up professional distance.

For us, the value of that distinction is accountability. Familiarity can make a source easier to reach, but it should not remove the questions that would be asked of anyone else. Audiences need reporting that helps them understand a situation, not merely a polished version of one participant’s preferred account.

A useful next step for aspiring storytellers

The episode connects career development with public responsibility. Becoming more fluent is only one part of the work; deciding what deserves publication and how it may affect an audience is another.

If you are starting out, choose one small, answerable question rather than a sweeping story. Identify the people and documents that could help answer it, then ask someone experienced to challenge the draft. That exercise is our recommendation inspired by the conversation.
